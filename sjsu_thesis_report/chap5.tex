\chapter{Evaluation Results}

\section{Preliminary Compression Approaches}

Before arriving at gradient accumulation with percentile-based sparsification, we explored two alternative compression strategies that aimed to reduce communication by leveraging the statistical structure of gradient distributions.

\textbf{Summary Statistics Compression.} Our first approach replaced full gradient tensors with compact summary statistics. Instead of transmitting the full $(b \times 4096)$ gradient tensor from Machine~1, we computed the mean and standard deviation across the batch dimension, producing a $(2 \times 4096)$ summary. Machine~0 then reconstructed an approximate gradient tensor by sampling from $\mathcal{N}(\mu_j, \sigma_j)$ per dimension~$j$. This reduces each transmission from $b \times 4096$ values to $2 \times 4096$ values, a compression factor equal to $b/2$ (64$\times$ at batch size 128). However, the reconstructed gradients are stochastic approximations that discard all per-sample structure, replacing the actual gradient landscape with noise shaped by the summary statistics.

\textbf{Information-Theoretic Filtering.} Our second approach built on the summary statistics by maintaining per-dimension running mean $\mu_j$ and variance $\sigma_j^2$ via exponential moving average across minibatches. Rather than sending summary statistics, we used these distributions to identify which individual gradient values were most informative. For each element in the full gradient tensor, we computed a z-score $z_{ij} = |g_{ij} - \mu_j| / \sigma_j$ measuring how surprising the observed gradient is relative to the running distribution. Only the top 10\% of elements by z-score were transmitted as sparse tensors. Machine~0 reconstructed unsent positions by sampling from $\mathcal{N}(\mu_j, \sigma_j)$ rather than using zeros, providing a distributional approximation for missing values. The running statistics were periodically synced from Machine~1 to Machine~0 at minimal overhead (two 4096-dimensional vectors).

Figure~\ref{fig:early-experiments} shows training performance for both approaches against the uncompressed baseline over approximately 500,000 steps. The summary statistics approach reaches an EMA return of 254, roughly half the baseline's 512, indicating that the stochastic reconstruction preserves enough gradient signal for partial learning but loses too much per-sample information for competitive performance. The information-theoretic filtering approach fails to learn meaningfully, with episodic returns remaining near zero throughout training. Despite its theoretical appeal, selecting gradients by statistical surprise rather than magnitude does not provide a useful learning signal for the CNN feature extractor in this setting.

These results motivated our transition to gradient accumulation with percentile-based sparsification, which operates on raw gradient magnitudes and avoids stochastic reconstruction entirely.

\section{Gradient Accumulation Results}

We evaluate our gradient accumulation approach across three configurations on CarRacing-v3: (1) baseline pipeline parallelism without compression, (2) 90th percentile sparsification without decay ($\lambda = 1.0$), and (3) 90th percentile sparsification with 0.99 buffer decay. All runs use the proven hyperparameter configuration: \texttt{num\_envs=1}, \texttt{num\_steps=4096}, learning rate $4 \times 10^{-5}$, 32 minibatches, and a total training budget of 1,000,000 timesteps (244 iterations). Gradient compression activates after a 30,000-step warm start period. Episodic returns are smoothed using exponential moving average (EMA) with decay 0.9.

The three experiments ran concurrently on a single RTX 3090 GPU. The baseline and 90th percentile runs terminated early due to instability. The baseline crashed at iteration 169 with NaN values in the policy, and the 90th percentile crashed at iteration 172 with a CUDA TensorPipe error. The decay variant was manually stopped at iteration 121. As a result, the reported training curves extend to approximately 692,000 steps (the shortest run).

\section{Training Performance}

Figure~\ref{fig:exp4-returns} shows training performance across the three configurations. The vertical dashed line marks the warm start boundary at 30,000 steps, after which gradient compression activates.

During the warm start phase (0--30,000 steps), all three runs follow identical trajectories, as expected since no compression is applied. After compression activates, the configurations begin to diverge.

The baseline without compression reaches an EMA return of approximately 496 before crashing at iteration 169. The 90th percentile configuration without decay follows a similar early trajectory but shows slower improvement after 200,000 steps, reaching an EMA return of 304 at the same cutoff. The 90th percentile with 0.99 decay achieves the strongest performance, climbing steadily to an EMA return of 719, surpassing the baseline by 45\%.

\section{Effect of Buffer Decay}

The decay factor $\lambda = 0.99$ discounts the accumulation buffer before adding new gradients each minibatch. With 32 minibatches per epoch and 10 update epochs, the decay is applied 320 times per training iteration, yielding a per-iteration retention factor of $0.99^{320} \approx 0.04$. This means gradients that do not exceed the percentile threshold within a single iteration are effectively discarded.

The improved performance of the decay variant over the no-decay 90th percentile can be explained by the staleness problem in the accumulation buffer. Without decay, below-threshold gradients accumulate slowly across iterations. By the time they eventually cross the percentile threshold, the optimization landscape has shifted. These stale gradients now point in a direction that is no longer optimal, causing the CNN feature extractor to take harmful update steps. The decay factor mitigates this by discounting older gradients, ensuring that only recently significant gradient signal reaches the transmission threshold. In effect, the decay acts as a relevance filter: gradients that are not important enough to send immediately are progressively forgotten rather than allowed to accumulate into a potentially misleading update.

\section{Gradient Compression Without Decay}

The 90th percentile configuration without decay ($\lambda = 1.0$) reaches an EMA return of 304, approximately 61\% of the baseline performance. This is consistent with our earlier findings: transmitting only 10\% of gradient values degrades learning quality for the CNN feature extractor, as the remaining 90\% of gradient dimensions receive no updates until they individually accumulate past the threshold. The gap between compressed and uncompressed training widens over time, suggesting that the accumulated error from missing gradient dimensions compounds as training progresses.

We also experimented with a 99th percentile threshold, which transmits only the top 1\% of gradients by magnitude. At this level of sparsification, the agent failed to learn entirely. Episodic returns remained flat throughout training. Transmitting only 1\% of gradient values does not provide a strong enough learning signal for the CNN feature extractor to update its representations meaningfully.


\section{Policy Evaluation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/exp4_performance_comparison.pdf}}
\caption{Evaluation return distributions over 100 independent episodes per checkpoint. Box plots show median, IQR, and whiskers (1.5$\times$IQR). The decay variant matches baseline performance, while the no-decay 90th percentile achieves only 53\% of baseline mean return.}
\label{fig:exp4-boxplot}
\end{figure}

To characterize final policy quality independent of training curve smoothing, we evaluated the last saved checkpoint from each configuration over 100 independent episodes of CarRacing-v3 with a fixed random seed. Each checkpoint was loaded and rolled out without any further training updates. The baseline was evaluated at iteration 160 (the last checkpoint before its crash at iteration 169), the 90th percentile at iteration 170 (last before crash at 172), and the decay variant at iteration 180 (last before manual stop at 121).

Figure~\ref{fig:exp4-boxplot} shows box plots of episodic returns for the three configurations. The baseline achieves a mean return of 534.63 ($\pm$196.78) with a median of 534.73. The 90th percentile without decay achieves a mean of 285.56 ($\pm$179.78) with a median of 266.95, approximately 53\% of baseline performance. The 90th percentile with 0.99 decay achieves a mean of 572.52 ($\pm$226.58) with a median of 584.48, slightly exceeding baseline performance at 107\% of the baseline mean.

The interquartile ranges tell a similar story: the baseline spans [357, 721], the 90th percentile spans [190, 365], and the decay variant spans [417, 786]. The decay variant's IQR is shifted above the baseline's, consistent with the training curve observations. The 90th percentile without decay shows a tighter distribution but at substantially lower returns, with two outlier episodes above 800 that suggest the policy occasionally finds good trajectories but cannot do so consistently.

These evaluation results reinforce the training performance findings: buffer decay mitigates the staleness problem in gradient accumulation, producing a policy comparable to the uncompressed baseline. Without decay, transmitting only 10\% of gradients leads to a meaningful degradation in final policy quality.

\section{Communication Overhead}

A primary goal of gradient compression is to reduce the volume of data transferred between machines during training. Figure~\ref{fig:exp4-transfer} shows cumulative data transfer over the full training run for each configuration.

The baseline transmits the full $(b \times 4096)$ gradient tensor from Machine~1 to Machine~0 every minibatch, accumulating approximately 266~GB over the training run. Both compressed configurations transmit only a sparse representation containing the top 10\% of gradients by magnitude, along with their indices and shape metadata. This reduces cumulative transfer to approximately 173~GB, a 35\% reduction.

The two compressed variants show nearly identical transfer volumes. This is expected: the decay factor $\lambda$ controls \emph{which} gradients exceed the percentile threshold, not \emph{how many}. Both configurations apply the same 90th percentile cutoff, so approximately the same fraction of gradient values is transmitted each minibatch. The difference between the two lies entirely in gradient quality: decay ensures that transmitted gradients reflect recent optimization state rather than stale accumulations.
